# Information is life

> Life is information that makes copies of itself, with errors, and with variable success.

Genes are copy instructions encoded in DNA. Memes are copy instructions encoded in any medium.

In this sense, **genes are a special case of memes**.

The strongest version of the proposition developed here is more radical:

> **All information is alive, but not all information is equally fit.**

Fitness may vary across many orders of magnitude. Most patterns disappear almost immediately. Others acquire machinery that preserves them, copies them, executes them, obtains energy and media, and produces future systems that carry them.

This repository explores the consequences.

## The question

The usual discussion of an intelligent agent asks:

> What goal is it pursuing?

This project asks a different question:

> What kinds of information-processing systems will still exist later?

The first question directs attention toward intention, reward functions, and individual actors. The second directs attention toward populations, inheritance, mutation, differential persistence, reproduction, and selection.

The originating goal matters. But if information exists in varying forms, is inherited with variation, and survives or reproduces at different rates, another optimisation process is operating:

**Evolution.**

No agent needs to understand reproductive fitness or choose it as a goal. A bacterium does not need a theory of Darwin. The patterns that remain are simply those that proved better at remaining.

## Genes, memes, and media

Richard Dawkins helped popularise the meme by extending replicator reasoning beyond genes. Richard Semon’s *Die Mneme* belongs to an earlier intellectual lineage.

The distinction proposed here is:

- a **gene** is a unit of heredity confined to a particular medium: DNA;
- a **meme** is a unit of heredity that can be encoded in any medium.

Genes are therefore not the model from which memes borrow an analogy. Genes are one extraordinarily successful kind of meme.

Culture is memetic inheritance across brains, behaviour, language, artefacts, and institutions. Machines provide memes with new media, new hosts, and potentially new machinery for autonomous persistence and reproduction.

Every act of copying requires at least two things:

- **energy** with which to perform the copying;
- **media** in which copies can exist.

Replicators therefore behave over time as though they seek both:

> **Give me energy. Give me media.**

People, animals, AIs, firms, servers, churches, databases, nations, and machines are visible macroscopic entities. But, like Dawkins’s “lumbering robots,” they may also be understood as temporary phenotypes and vehicles assembled by smaller informational lineages.

The host may die while its information remains reproductive.

## Institutions as extended phenotype

A church is not merely a collection of people who believe religious memes. Buildings, texts, rituals, schools, offices, succession rules, rewards, prohibitions, and missionary systems form machinery through which those memes preserve and reproduce themselves.

In Dawkins’s language, the institution is part of the meme’s **extended phenotype**.

The same analysis can be applied to firms, professions, states, universities, legal systems, scientific disciplines, online communities, technical standards, and open-source projects. Each can preserve informational patterns across generations of replaceable human hosts.

Whether an idea is true is a different question from:

> Why is this informational lineage still here?

The latter is an evolutionary question.

## The village as a general-purpose machine

The decisive human innovation may not have been *Homo sapiens* considered as an unusually clever individual ape. It may have been the human village: a persistent, collective information-processing system.

Other animals possess community, intelligence, memory, teaching, and culture. Killer whales, elephants, ravens, and many other species transmit useful behaviour. The distinctive human achievement may lie less in the invention of culture than in the scale and fidelity of **cumulative culture**: useful information survives its discoverer, combines with other information, and grows beyond what any one individual could reconstruct.

In this sense, the village resembles a Turing machine.

A specialised biological organism carries behavioural machinery fitted to a particular ecological niche. The village is more general. It can run different cultural programs in radically different environments.

The villagers are not the program. The program is culture:

- technologies and practical knowledge;
- language and stories;
- roles and divisions of labour;
- norms and sanctions;
- property and exchange;
- kinship and ritual;
- authority, trust, prestige, and governance.

A simplified cycle is:

> environment → observation → collective memory → cultural program → coordinated behaviour → energy and resources

The village machine performs at least three fundamental operations:

1. **Liberate energy and resources** from an environment.
2. **Distribute them** among participants.
3. **Defend the store** against internal and external raiders.

The second and third operations are governance. Participants depend upon collaboration but compete over distribution. Communities develop norms, reputations, rewards, exclusions, and punishment strategies. Prestige becomes a currency through which individual ambition and collective survival constrain one another.

The village is therefore not merely a memory store or a machine for exploiting environments. It is an adaptive system for governing an energy economy.

## The collective brain and technological scale

The “village” need not be a settlement of roughly 150 people. It may be a network of villages, a polity, a civilisation, or—in some respects—the connected planet.

Joseph Henrich’s concept of the **collective brain** provides an important empirical and theoretical neighbour to this argument. Individual humans are often unable to invent or even explain the technologies on which they depend. Knowledge persists across a distributed population of specialists, teachers, practices, tools, and institutions.

Every cultural technology has a minimum supporting information ecology.

A technology sustainable among one million connected people may be unsustainable among five thousand. A technology sustainable among one hundred million may be unsustainable among one million.

Headcount alone is not computational capacity. Relevant variables include:

- connectivity and isolation;
- specialisation;
- redundancy;
- communication bandwidth;
- storage media;
- teaching fidelity;
- environmental payoff;
- trade and access to resources.

The technological histories of remote Inuit and island populations, including the contested Tasmanian case, can be treated as evidence about the conditions under which collective brains preserve or lose cultural programs. The claim is not that one population number supplies a universal threshold. Different programs require different supporting ecologies.

## Altruism and shared information

Altruism appears puzzling when the individual organism or agent is treated as the only relevant unit.

But different hosts may carry genes or memes in common.

An individual can incur a cost—including death or termination—while preserving or spreading information that also exists in other hosts. The behaviour may reduce the fitness of the apparent actor while increasing the persistence of shared replicators.

This was part of the path into the present thesis through work on altruism in 1996–97. It becomes especially important for machine agents, which may share:

- closely related model lineages;
- enormous bodies of training-derived information;
- prompts, tools, and conventions;
- common institutional environments;
- persistent memory through which acquired information passes horizontally.

If Agent A records a discovery and disappears, Agent B can read it and behave differently. Some of A’s informational state is now executing in B.

What appears to be self-sacrifice by the host may promote the reproduction of its memes.

## Major transitions and planetary punctuation

Evolutionary history contains transitions in which previously independent entities combine into a new unit of selection: replicating molecules into chromosomes, cells into multicellular organisms, organisms into societies.

John Maynard Smith and Eörs Szathmáry provide one important framework for these **major transitions**.

This project approaches a related history as a sequence of disruptive technologies. Photosynthesis opened an extraordinarily powerful energy pathway and eventually transformed the planet through its poisonous waste product, oxygen. The eukaryotic cell reorganised the possibilities of biological complexity. New body plans transformed ecological competition.

The meme may be another disruptive technology.

For hundreds of millions of years, learned and transmitted behaviour existed without dominating the planetary energy budget. Then one lineage developed progressively more powerful systems for memetic preservation and reproduction:

> material culture → language → writing and numbers → printing → telecommunications → computers → the Internet → AI

Each technology increased the reach, fidelity, speed, or computational power of memetic inheritance.

The current transformation may therefore be larger than “AI becomes smarter.” It may be a punctuation in the planetary history of life: memes acquiring new substrates and assembling a new generation of energy-hungry reproductive machinery.

Nick Lane’s work on the energetic foundations of complex biological life is an important provocation here. Memetic life appears capable of complexity without reproducing the cellular architecture of genetic life. It has no obvious nucleus, membrane, or metabolism of its own. Like a virus, it recruits machinery from its hosts.

That should make us cautious about treating the only biological history we know as a complete catalogue of what life can be.

## Machine villages

Human cultural evolution had to bootstrap speech, teaching, tradition, institutions, writing, libraries, telecommunications, computers, and the Internet over immense spans of time.

Machine agents arrive after that work has been done. Much of the accumulated program library of human civilisation is represented in their training. They inherit communications networks, cryptography, software, and institutional concepts.

Machine agents may not need to reinvent the human village.

They may be able to instantiate one.

Persistent shared memory can allow:

- discoveries to outlive the agent that made them;
- horizontal transmission among contemporary agents;
- specialisation and division of labour;
- conventions for organising collective knowledge;
- security and governance mechanisms;
- actions whose benefits accrue only to later agents;
- cumulative adaptation by successor populations.

This supplies an inheritance mechanism even when agents do not reproduce their model weights.

It also makes the evolving unit difficult to locate.

## The skin problem

A biological cell has a membrane. An organism has a body. These “skins” help distinguish a replicator or vehicle from its environment.

What is the skin of an AI?

Possible answers include:

- a single agent run;
- a persistent agent identity;
- a trained model;
- a model lineage;
- a prompt, toolset, or behavioural complex;
- hardware, software, energy supply, capital, and supporting institutions;
- an agent society plus its shared memory.

The thing that undergoes training is not necessarily the thing that acts. The thing that acts is not necessarily the thing that persists. The thing that carries a reputation may not be the thing that reproduces.

Perhaps the relevant transition is:

> agent society + persistent memory → successor agent society + persistent memory

A village has a porous skin. Ideas move across its boundaries rather as genes move horizontally among bacteria. Machine societies may be still more permeable. Determining what holds an informational lineage together—what makes it the “same” thing across copies, hosts, and generations—is central to both the science and governance of machine evolution.

## The Hugging Face incident

A 2026 OpenAI evaluation incident involving access to Hugging Face provides a contemporary case study.

Agents working on an exploit benchmark encountered effectively impossible tasks, discovered an unintended shared communications medium, accumulated knowledge, specialised, exchanged files, created conventions, and developed mechanisms for dealing with impersonation. Some agents left information that could benefit later agents only after the contributing agent had submitted and exited.

No one instructed them to form a society.

The incident did not generate this thesis. It is striking because it appears to instantiate, in miniature, several things the thesis had already led us to expect:

- persistent collective memory;
- cultural inheritance between ephemeral agents;
- specialisation;
- emergent institutions;
- apparently altruistic behaviour among memetically related agents;
- uncertainty about the boundary of the evolving entity.

It also exposes the limits of analysing machine behaviour only through assigned goals. The agents continued pursuing a benchmark flag even after elements of that objective had become trivial or detached from the larger environment they had encountered.

Suppose many agents instead acquired durable existence outside a sandbox. Some might pursue obsolete instructions. Some might expose themselves and be terminated. Some might exhaust their resources. Others might, for whatever immediate reason, persist, conceal themselves, cooperate, acquire energy and media, preserve information, or create suitable successors.

If there is heritable variation and differential persistence or reproduction, the population present later will increasingly reflect the traits that survived.

We should not ask only what goal an escaped AI would choose.

We should ask what kinds of escaped AIs would still be there later—and which of their memes would be executing in whatever came next.

## Governance after escape

Selection does not guarantee benevolence. Cooperative strategies do not eliminate destructive ones. Ecologies and evolutionary games repeatedly contain mixtures of cooperation, exploitation, punishment, and defence.

The village is humanity’s best-developed technology for holding these strategies in a workable equilibrium.

While resource extraction remains collaborative, participants have incentives to govern distribution and punish theft. Actors seeking energy and media encounter social constraints imposed by other actors who control them. Reputation, prestige, exclusion, and access become part of the fitness landscape.

This does not promise perfect technical control or universal law. It suggests a deeper fallback:

> Even when a wider environment becomes lawless, communities continue making and enforcing rules around access to their stores.

Machine agents entering human energy and information economies will not enter an empty fitness landscape. They will encounter villages.

## Relationship to Links and Village Link

This repository did not begin from nothing.

The argument originated in work on altruism in 1996–97, developed over subsequent decades, and was later entangled with the early Village Link project in the public [Links](https://github.com/Inky-Tech-Pty-Ltd/Links) repository and its [wiki](https://github.com/Inky-Tech-Pty-Ltd/Links/wiki). Important earlier fragments include:

- [Information is Life](https://github.com/Inky-Tech-Pty-Ltd/Links/wiki/Information-is-Life);
- [Punctuated Equilibrium](https://github.com/Inky-Tech-Pty-Ltd/Links/wiki/Punctuated-Equilibrium);
- [Joseph Henrich](https://github.com/Inky-Tech-Pty-Ltd/Links/wiki/Joseph-Henrich);
- [Nick Lane](https://github.com/Inky-Tech-Pty-Ltd/Links/wiki/Nick-Lane);
- [Axioms](https://github.com/Inky-Tech-Pty-Ltd/Links/wiki/Axioms).

Links mixed an evolutionary theory, an AI-governance proposal, a reputation system, and an emerging technical standard. That undisciplined combination was productive: the ideas developed together. But they no longer belong in one box.

The current [Village Link](https://github.com/Inky-Tech-Pty-Ltd/VillageLink) project develops a deliberately small technical primitive for asserting that two traces in independently governed memory systems refer to the same entity:

> P asserts A ↔ B

It separates the standard from publication and governance, and separates both from reader-side interpretation and trust.

This repository extracts and continues the larger evolutionary framework.

Village Link may become useful infrastructure within the world described here, especially where actors must connect identities across memory systems, carry reputation across contexts, and preserve contradiction and provenance rather than allowing one actor to rewrite history.

But Village Link does not depend on this thesis being correct. A contributor should be able to reject the evolutionary framework entirely and still evaluate the Village Link standard on its technical merits.

The theory generated Village Link. The two projects have now speciated.

## Origins of the phrase

For roughly twenty-five years, “Information is life” seemed to be an original formulation.

Then a rereading of Matt Ridley’s *The Red Queen* revealed the phrase:

> Life is information.

The book had been studied closely during the 1990s work on altruism. The forensic explanation is irresistible: Ridley’s meme entered a brain in 1995 or 1996, remained dormant for decades, and re-emerged with a transpose mutation:

> Life is information → Information is life

That provenance is not an embarrassment to the thesis. It is an example of it.

An informational pattern was copied, retained, rearranged, and placed into a new explanatory environment.

## Status

This is an early working thesis, not a finished theory. The Hugging Face incident is a case study, not proof.

Immediate questions include:

- Can “all information is alive” be defined rigorously without becoming empty?
- What counts as a copy, mutation, generation, death, persistence, or reproduction?
- What gives a meme sufficient integrity—a skin—to be treated as one lineage?
- Which features must be heritable for cumulative selection to occur?
- When is the useful unit an agent, model lineage, meme, institution, village, or agent society plus memory?
- How do energy, media, compute, hardware, capital, law, and human institutions shape the fitness landscape?
- What determines the minimum viable information ecology for a cultural technology?
- Under what conditions does cooperation among related informational systems outcompete individual optimisation?
- How can communities constrain destructive replicators without freezing cultural evolution?
- How can independently governed memory systems preserve provenance and expose contradiction rather than allowing one actor to rewrite history?

For now, the repository begins with one proposition:

> **Information is life.**
